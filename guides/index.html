Copy guides for junior writers

Four short guides to how I write for a brand: the do’s, the don’ts, and prompts you can copy and paste.

Mehr Patni · Senior writer · Dubai · mehrpatni.com

How to prompt

Every prompt has five lines.

Role

Who the AI should be.

EX: Senior conversion copywriter
Reader

Who it is writing for.

EX: A founder worried about price
Facts

What it may use. Nothing else.

EX: Your numbers, reviews and notes
Voice

How it sounds, and what it never says.

EX: Plain and warm. Never “seamless”
Output

Exactly what to hand back.

EX: 5 headlines and 1 call to action
One line

Gets you an average answer, in an average voice.

Five lines

Gets you a draft you can actually use.

Hot take

The prompt is the brief. If a human junior could not write from it, the AI cannot either.

Website

Say what changes for the customer. Then prove it.

Landing page

Sells one promise

  • Headline: the outcome, not the product. The subhead says who it is for.
  • Proof: a number, a name or a real review, right beside the claim.
  • Benefits: three at most, in your customer’s own words.
  • One ask: a single button that says what happens next.

Product description

Answers the last doubt

  • Open with the benefit, then back it with the fact.
  • Answer doubts: size, fit, care, delivery, returns.
  • Sensory words beat adjectives. “Soft” beats “premium”.
  • Check claims with legal. Even famous lines get challenged.
Hot take

“Premium”, “innovative” and “seamless” are not copy, they are placeholders. When I see one in a draft, I ask what we actually mean.

Landing page

You are a senior conversion copywriter. Write a landing page for [PRODUCT].

Reader: [WHO THEY ARE], who is worried about [OBJECTION].

Use only these facts: [FACTS, NUMBERS, REVIEWS]. If a claim is not backed
by them, flag it instead of writing it.

Voice: [3 ADJECTIVES]. Never use: [BANNED WORDS].

Give me: 5 headlines that state an outcome, 1 subhead, 3 benefits with
proof, and 1 call to action that says what happens next.

Product description

You are a senior e-commerce copywriter. Write a product description
for [PRODUCT].

Customer: [WHO], who is choosing between this and [ALTERNATIVE].
Their biggest doubt: [DOUBT].

Use only these facts: [SPECS, MATERIALS, SIZES, REVIEWS]. Turn each into
what it means for the customer. Flag anything you cannot support.

Voice: [3 ADJECTIVES]. Never use: "premium", "high-quality",
"perfect for any occasion".

Give me: a one-line headline, a 40-word description, 4 bullets (benefit
first, fact second) and one line that answers the doubt.

Not this: “Write a landing page for my product. Make it catchy and professional.” It gives the model nothing to work with, so you get clichés.

Social media

Win the first line. The rest only has to keep the promise.

Instagram

Three seconds

  • Reels: put the payoff on screen and out loud, straight away.
  • Captions: the point goes in the first line. The feed cuts off the rest.
  • Proof: show the real thing. A face, a number, the product.
  • One post, one point. A second takeaway is a second post.

LinkedIn

A few lines

  • Hook: a result, a mistake, a contrarian take or a question.
  • Shape: short lines, one idea each, a blank line between.
  • Story: the situation, the turn, the lesson.
  • Ending: one question your reader can answer in a line.
Hot take

If your hook could open any brand’s post, it is not a hook. Make it so specific that only you could have written it.

Draft the post

You are a social writer for [BRAND] on Instagram and LinkedIn.

Reader: [WHO], who is stuck on [PROBLEM].

Angle: [ONE INSIGHT, STORY OR RESULT]. Use only these notes: [PASTE NOTES].

Voice: [3 ADJECTIVES]. Never use: "excited to share", "game-changer",
"unlock".

Give me: 5 openers under 12 words, a LinkedIn post (short lines, under
[180] words, ending on one question) and an Instagram caption (point in
the first line).

Sharpen the hook

Here is my draft post: [PASTE].

Rewrite only the first line 5 ways: a result, a mistake, a contrarian
take, a question and a specific moment.

Score each from 1 to 5 on: specific, surprising, would I stop scrolling?

Pick the best and say why in one sentence. Do not touch the rest.

Not this: “Write a LinkedIn post about our new feature.” There is no reader and no angle, so expect “Excited to announce…”.

Brand voice

A voice is not adjectives. It is decisions.

The voice stays the same. The tone shifts with the moment.

Ask the founder

Before you write

  • Beliefs: start with what they believe and who they serve, not a mood board.
  • Nevers: ask what they would never say, and who they hate sounding like.
  • Samples: collect five pieces of copy they are proud of.
  • Specifics: push past “friendly and professional”.

Write it down

While you write

  • The “not”: give every trait one. Warm, not chatty.
  • Sentences: show each trait in a before-and-after line.
  • Hard moments: plan the error, the apology and the refund.
  • One page: a new writer should be able to use it today.
Hot take

A brand voice is what you refuse to say. The “nots” do more work than the adjectives.

Then ask about moments

  • How should we sound when a customer is angry?
  • When we launch something big?
  • When we make a mistake?
  • When we explain something complicated?

Send this to the founder

  • If our brand walked into a room, who would it be? Three words.
  • What do we believe that our category does not?
  • Who do we talk to, and what worries them at 11pm?
  • Which brand’s voice do you love, and which would you hate to sound like?
  • Which words would we never say?
Hot take

Never accept “somewhere in between”. Middle of the road is how a brand ends up sounding like nobody.

DoDon’t
The classic · Mailchimp’s voice guide
“We are plainspoken. We are genuine. Our humor is dry.”
Traits a writer can act on. Each one tells you what to do next.
The usual version
“We’re friendly and professional.”
Personality · Oatly
“Yes, we are vegan. So?”
Personality
“We are proud to offer plant-based options for conscious consumers.”
Voice vs tone · Mailchimp
Same voice every day. A different tone for someone who is upset.
Voice vs tone
One tone, everywhere, all the time.
Many markets
Same voice, local words: “lift” in London, “elevator” in New York.
Many markets
The same copy, translated word for word, in every market.

Turn the answers into a voice guide

You are a brand strategist and senior writer. Here are the founder's
answers: [PASTE] and five pieces of copy they love: [PASTE].

Find the three traits that show up in both. For each trait write: what it
sounds like, "we are X, not Y", a before-and-after sentence, three words
we use and three we avoid.

Then add tone for a launch, an error and an apology.

Quote the founder's own words. Flag any answers that contradict each other.

Not this: “Create a tone of voice guide for our brand. We’re friendly and professional.” Every brand says that, so nothing tells a writer what to do.

Your voice

You already have a voice. Notice it, then make it deliberate.

Read your own writing like an editor who has never met you.

Notice

Step one

  • Aloud: read it out. Where you stumble, the sentence is wrong.
  • Length: count the words in your last five sentences.
  • Tics: list the words you overuse. “Just”, “really”, “very”.
  • Signature: find the three lines that sound most like you.

Edit

Step two

  • Rhythm: put a short sentence next to a long one.
  • Hedges: cut “just”, “I think” and the warm-up.
  • Plain words: if a short word works, use it.
  • Keep: the habits that sound like you. Break the rest.
Hot take

You do not need to “find your voice”. You need to stop sanding it down.

DoDon’t
The classic · Avis, 1962
“We try harder.”
Three words, an honest admission and a promise. Cut until it still sounds like you.
The usual version
“We are committed to continually improving our customer experience.”
Hedges
Can we talk Thursday?
Hedges
I just wanted to quickly reach out to see if you might have some time to connect.
Opener
Hi Sam, loved your talk on pricing.
Opener
I hope this email finds you well.
Bio
I write product pages that sell and onboarding emails people finish.
Bio
Passionate marketer with a proven track record of driving results.

Answer these first

  • Read one piece aloud. Where do you stumble?
  • Count the words in your last five sentences. Short, long or mixed?
  • Do you plan first, or write to find out what you think?
  • Which words do you overuse?
  • What do you always delete in the edit?
  • Whose writing do you copy, on purpose or by accident?

Let AI read your samples

You are an editor who reads for voice, not grammar. Here are five samples
of my writing: [PASTE]. Do not rewrite them.

Describe my voice: average sentence length, words and openers I repeat,
how formal I am, how I use questions.

Quote the 3 lines that sound most like me and the 3 that sound least,
and say why.

Then give me 5 habits to keep and 3 to break. Evidence over praise.

Not this: “Analyse my writing style.” The model guesses, so you get flattery and a list of adjectives.

Checklist

Before it ships.

Website

  • Headline states the outcome
  • Every claim has proof
  • One ask, one clear verb
  • Product details match the product
  • Read aloud: sounds human

Social media

  • First line works alone
  • One idea, nothing extra
  • Cut for the platform
  • Ends on a real question
  • Facts and tags checked

Brand voice

  • Every trait has a “not”
  • Real before-and-after lines
  • Hard moments planned
  • Fits on one page
  • Founder says “that’s us”

Your voice

  • Read aloud, no stumbles
  • Short next to long
  • Hedges and filler cut
  • Still sounds like you
  • Cut the first line: still works?
Hot take

AI can draft it. Only a person can check it. Every fact, price and claim gets read by a human.
